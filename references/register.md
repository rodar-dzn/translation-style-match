# Register and voice

The axis that decides whether a draft is *plausible* or *indistinguishable*.
Typography and glossary are enforceable by rule. This one is not, and it is
where most of a review pass should go.

## What register actually is

A character's voice is a bundle of measurable habits. Strip the attribution
from a line of dialogue; if you still know who is speaking, the bundle is
working. If you do not, it has been flattened.

The bundle is built from:

| Signal | What varies |
|---|---|
| **Lexical stratum** | archaic / literary / neutral / colloquial / coarse |
| **Syntax** | period length, subordination depth, inversion, ellipsis |
| **Sentence length** | and its variance — some voices are uniform, some lurch |
| **Profanity** | frequency, placement, whether it carries meaning or rhythm |
| **Terms of address** | formal/informal pronouns, honorifics, diminutives |
| **Marker words** | a particle or tic recurring only in one character's speech |
| **Domain vocabulary** | trade, faith, war, court — what they reach for |
| **Register mixing** | whether a voice stays in one stratum or collides two |

## Profiling a voice from the corpus

For each recurring character, collect twenty to thirty lines of their speech
from the reference translation. Then answer, in writing:

1. Which stratum dominates, and where does it break?
2. What is the mean sentence length, and is it steady or erratic?
3. Is there a marker word? How often does it appear — and is it doing
   grammatical work, or rhythmic work?
4. How do they address other characters, and does it change by interlocutor?
5. What do they *never* do? Absences are as characteristic as presences.

Write the answers into the style guide as a short profile per character.
This is the artifact you translate against.

## Register mixing: the device worth naming

The most distinctive voices usually collide two strata that do not belong
together — formal vocabulary in a coarse mouth, or gutter idiom from a
character whose position implies polish. The collision *is* the
characterization, and it is exactly what gets lost in careless translation,
because a translator working line by line will normalize each sentence
toward the middle.

Invented illustration. A soldier-turned-cleric whose speech mixes liturgical
vocabulary with barracks profanity:

```
Flattened:   – Я не собираюсь этого делать. Это плохая идея.
Mixed:       – Не стану я, к чертям, творить этакую мерзость.
```

Both are grammatical. Only the second carries a person. The flattened
version is what you get when register is not being tracked as a variable.

If you find such a collision in the corpus, name it explicitly in the style
guide, with three or four cited examples. It will be the single most useful
line in the document.

## Marker words

A particle, oath, or filler recurring in one character's speech and nowhere
else. Three rules:

- **Preserve frequency, not just presence.** A marker used once a page in
  the corpus and once a chapter in the draft has been lost even though it
  appears in both.
- **Preserve placement.** Markers usually attach to a fixed syntactic slot.
  Moving them reads as a different person with a similar habit.
- **Do not spread them.** A marker that migrates to other characters stops
  being a marker. This is a common drift in long drafts and worth grepping
  for directly.

## Narration versus speech

Profile the narrator as a voice too. Where a novel is narrated by a
character, the same bundle applies — and the interesting question is whether
the narrator's written register differs from their spoken one. Many books
depend on that gap. If the corpus maintains it, the draft must too.

Also record: does narration carry the same foreign layer as dialogue, or is
it cleaner? Does profanity survive into narration? These are policy
questions the corpus has already answered.

## Reviewing for register

Read a chapter with the character profiles open. For each line of dialogue,
ask only: *could this be the other character?* Where the answer is yes, you
have a finding.

Report it with the span quoted, the signal that is missing, and a specific
rewrite:

> **Register — [character].** «…» reads as neutral narration. This
> character's speech in the corpus mixes [stratum A] with [stratum B]
> (cited: …). Suggested: «…»

Do not report register findings as "make this more distinctive". That is a
direction, not a fix, and it puts the work back on the translator without
adding information.

## The flattening test

A quick whole-draft check before detailed reading: extract every dialogue
line, strip attributions, shuffle, and try to assign speakers. The
proportion you can assign confidently is a rough measure of how much voice
survived. Run the same test on the reference corpus for a baseline — the gap
between the two numbers is the size of the problem.
